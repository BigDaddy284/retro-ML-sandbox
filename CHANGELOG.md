# Changelog

All notable changes are recorded here, one entry per completed task. Format loosely follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/).

## [Unreleased]

### Added
- T-1023: going back to a saved moment no longer costs the AI a step of the ground it counts. Its eyes used to start afresh when a go was put back, so the screen's slide in the step after was measured against nothing and lost; a go goes back several times a life, and the ground counted matched the game's own in 2 and 3 goes of 12 on two practice levels. They now pick up from the screen of the moment gone back to (`ObjectTracker.start_from`, the screen carried in the watch's `Mark`), and it matches in 12 of 12 on both; with the neural network's longer goes in 52% where it was 12%, so the target of 90% is met for the one and not yet for the other. On sixteen levels the AI finished 151 levels where it finished 133 and lost 92 lives where it lost 113 (one run each). Nothing is read from the game's memory across a going back, as before.
- T-1021: a landing is no longer taken for the game starting over. After a jump the fall speed and the "on the ground" value are back where they began, together, and early in a go, when little else has moved, that met the whole rule for a restart: goes ended after a handful of steps, three to six in a row. The AI now keeps count of the values each thing it follows comes to while most of the others do not change at all (never in the first snapshot after the app itself loads a saved state: the emulator adapter counts its loads and that snapshot is marked). A value seen coming to its starting value that way three times is named once in the log, and a restart needs at least one value going home that is not like that. Such values still count with the others. Three simpler ways of counting were built and dropped first, because each ended up treating the life count, the score or the "game over" switch the same way; the last two were caught by the first demonstration's test on the racing game.
- T-1020: the AI reads how far the screen slid right where the picture alone cannot tell. On bare ground with one enemy walking on it, a blank stretch or a pattern that repeats, the picture has one number for two unknowns, and the camera's move was misread in about one moving look in five (16 pixels read as 20, as 12 or as none). The AI now finds by itself which number in the game's memory moves as the screen slides (`CameraMemory`: twenty looks read as moving and twenty as still, a byte agreeing in 6 of 10 of each), says so once in the log, and the tracker takes that number's word only where the new screen fits it as well as it fits the picture's own answer (`ObjectTracker.update(..., expect=)`); where the picture can tell, the picture decides. Nothing about any game is written down, and a game with no such number loses nothing. On the practice game the camera's move is read right in 100% of looks against 80% to 83%, and the ground counted matches the game's own in 12% of the neural network's goes against none (17% and 25% of the simple table's against 8% and none); what is left of that is the first look after the game is put back. One older test's bound (T-1018's standstill test), which held only while the camera was misread, is corrected by one step with Boss's word. The tries of a search still measure by the picture alone.
- T-1018: a go that comes to a wall no longer stands there until it runs out. Where a go that has got somewhere stays about one spot for 60 steps with no life lost (or is about to end "stuck"), it goes back to before it came there and looks for a way on, as it does after losing a life; the way found starts as it reaches the spot, so the next go, which remembers it, does not wait there. A way a search has found is now played to its end before the go may end for its length. At a standstill the changes are tried from the start of what it did forwards, with a hold played out in full even past the end of it, and a way on must end out of the spot it stood about (more than 64 pixels from it), so the way has no waiting in it and a small hop is not taken for one. A try at a standstill is played blind first and watched only if it ends on a screen no try that got nowhere ended on, which made one such search take 8 seconds where it took 50. Standstill searches stop for the session after as many in a row find nothing as the searches after a life lost do (eight in the app), counted apart. The practice bench now counts apart the deaths that had only begun when a go ended and cost no life. The go's line and the bench say how many searches were made at a standstill; the bench's `--no-standstill` switches them off for comparing.
- T-1017: tried and not kept. Leaving the things known to move out of the measure of how far the screen scrolled made that measure worse on the practice game (right in 76% of looks where it had been 92%), so nothing in the app changed. Counting where the measure goes wrong showed that three quarters of its mistakes are where a walking enemy is the only thing in view on plain ground, which the picture alone cannot settle; the task's record says what would. Four tests now hold what the measure does near a moving thing.
- T-1016: the AI now sees an enemy that walks on the ground as a whole thing, and keeps seeing it when it stands still, so a life lost to it is put down to touching it. Before, on the practice platform game, none of the lives lost to a walking enemy were (0 of 28 and 0 of 17 in the long runs) and no kind of thing was ever found to hurt: standing on the ground the enemy was seen only by the thin edge that changed as it moved, and where it was the only thing in view the measure of the camera's move swallowed its move and it was not seen at all. The moving part is now filled out by its colours to the whole thing, taken only if that patch really stood elsewhere a look ago (so scenery an enemy walks in front of is not taken for it), and the kind is from then on known by its colours wherever it is on the screen. Measured over 40 long goes on each practice level: 49 of 49 and 14 of 15 lives lost to a walking enemy are now put down to it (99% of all lives lost explained on both, 70% and 79% before), and the enemy is found to be something that hurts, which the brain is then told the whereabouts of. One practice level is now finished from the third go on where it was the sixth. Still open: the camera's move is misjudged where only such a thing is in view; a thing that never moves and stands on the ground is not seen; the player finder does not use the new seeing.
- T-1015: the AI now begins each go by playing the best go it has played so far, by the rewards you set, and practises from where that ends. It is switched on in training wherever the AI can go back (an emulator with save states). Measured on the practice game with 40 goes on each of two levels: it reaches the end of the level from its sixth go on one and its third go on the other, and in every go that reaches it, it gets there before losing any life (without this the same goes got 989 and 1,106 of 2,496 on average and ended on game over). The simple brain, which never got beyond the first quarter of a level, reaches the end too. The steps played from memory do not count towards a go's length, so a long way cannot use the go up. What counts as the best go is what your rewards paid, no longer how much new ground it covered: on the practice racing game, where getting far is not the point, the furthest way led to a dead end and the AI replayed it go after go. For the same reason a way is cut back when a life goes within a few steps of its end and no way past is found. Two goes that begin with the same way are no longer taken for a game that ignores the AI. Pressing Start again, or starting the AI from scratch, forgets the way. The log says when the way grows and how many steps each go began with. Not yet: the way is not kept when the app is closed, and it is played back with every detour the go made. With goes reaching further, the eyes' known gaps further into a level show more (the screen's scrolling is misjudged where only a walking enemy is in view; a life lost to an enemy is not put down to it): that is the next work.
- T-1014: the AI can remember the furthest way it has played and begin a go by playing it again (the part that plays the game; it is switched on in training in the next task). Measured with goes long enough to finish a level, every go ended on the game-over screen: a go may go back and search three times, nothing found in one go helped the next, and so each go spent its three searches on the same first three places. Now the runner can keep the moves of the go that counted the most new ground, up to where it last got somewhere new, and the next go, which starts from the same saved spot, begins by playing them: it arrives at the end of what it knows alive, with its searches unused. A loss that stands ends what is remembered thirty steps before it; a life lost while the remembered way is being played cuts the way there; a step somebody else drove ends it; a new starting spot forgets it. A list of moves being played is no longer ended by the game standing still. In a try-out outside the app this finished the first practice level in the seventh go and then played it through without losing a life in every go after.
- T-1013: a life lost that also reads as the game starting over is searched before the go ends. On the first practice level 19 of 40 goes ended at the first enemy with two of their three searches unused. Followed step by step, the cause was the order of two things: the game puts the player back at the start when a life goes, the app (once it has learned which values to watch) reads that as the game starting over, in the same step, and ended the go there before it got to going back. The better it knew the game, the less it went back. Now, where a search can follow, the end of the go waits for it: a way found and the go carries on from before the loss; none found and the go ends as it did. Everything else that ends a go is unchanged. Measured over 40 goes: the first level 1,020 then 936 (950 on average over all 40 goes, 745 before; 3 goes ended that way, 19 before), the gaps level 861 then 1,413 (1,074 on average, 712 before; best 2,020 of the 2,496 to the flag, 1,020 before). Most goes now end because their 300 steps are up.
- T-1012: the search for a way past tries the plain way first. A third of the searches after a life lost found nothing (54 of 179 on the two practice levels). Written down one by one, nearly all of those had been given a wander: the brain had gone back a little, jumped too early or held a button that does nothing before the life went, and no single small change to a wander gets across a gap. Now the search begins by forgetting what the AI did: it holds the button that has paid best from the saved moment, and where that loses the life it tries the small changes on that. Only then, with at least half the tries left, does it try small changes to what the AI itself did, which is still what gets past where an earlier jump has to stay in. Measured over 40 goes: a way found in 96% of searches on the first level and 89% on the gaps level (74% and 66% before), with about 40% fewer tries in all; the first level 833 then 996 (745 on average over all 40 goes, 605 before), the gaps level 826 then 704 (712 on average, 588 before; no go stopped short of the first gap, 7 before). Exchanging one button for another, the fix first thought of, was measured and set aside: it solves a quarter as much at three times the tries. The log line of a way says which kind it was.
- T-1011: a brain turned timid presses on. Late in training on the gaps level the AI stopped a few steps short of the first gap and waited: it had lost lives there, it rates a step of waiting at 99% of what going on is worth, and so waiting won by a hair, for ever; it lost no lives, searched for no way past and got nowhere (the last ten of 40 goes reached 212 on average, the first ten 417). Now, when a go has been paid nothing for 24 steps and can still go back, it holds the button that has paid best so far for 16 steps; a go about to end "stuck" does the same first. A life lost to that is searched like any other, and the go carries on beyond. A press that got nothing is followed by the next-best-paid button. Measured over 40 goes: the gaps level 599 then 512 (all 40 goes 588 on average, 392 before; goes that never passed the first gap 7, 21 before), the first level 703 then 574 (605 on average, 463 before). It costs lives: about one a go on both levels. The go's line in the log says how often it pressed on.
- T-1010: sixth step of working out a way past where it died, last part: it is switched on. On an emulator with save states, training now keeps saved moments while the AI plays (in six slots of its own, `back0` to `back5`), and when a life is lost it goes back a little, plays the last seconds again with small changes at the emulator's top speed, and carries on from a way that got past. Up to three searches a go, 15 seconds each, and it stops going back in a game after eight searches in a row for nothing. The log says once that it will, and every go's line says how often it went back and what it found. The practice bench reports it too ("Ways past: ..."). Measured on the practice game over 40 goes: on the first level the AI reached 410 then 440 on average (242 then 194 before) and its best go 912 (449 before), on the gaps level 417 then 212 (222 then 151 before) with a best of 1,197 (244 before); a way was found in 62% and 57% of searches. The racing game's "different rewards make it play differently" test now walks six learner seeds instead of three, with its claims unchanged, because the search adds luck to a single training.
- T-1009: sixth step of working out a way past where it died, fourth part: going back gives up in a game where it finds nothing. Tried on the racing practice game before switching it on, a search there found a way 11 times in about 270, because the eyes count hardly any new ground in that game, and training took five times as long for it. A go now counts the searches in a row that found nothing, across goes; after so many it stops going back in that game, saves no more moments, and says so once in the log. A way found starts the count again. Switching it on in training, with that count set, is next.
- T-1008: sixth step of working out a way past where it died, third part: a search has a time limit and says how long it took. On a real emulator every try costs real time, and Stop has to wait for a search in flight; with a limit no new try is started once the time is up, and the loss stands as with any search that found nothing. The log line of a search now says how long it took, and when it ran out of time, that it did. Measured beforehand with the search switched on by hand, 40 goes on each practice level: the AI got about twice as far (410 from 242 and 417 from 222 in its first ten goes; best go 912 from 449 and 1,197 from 244). Switching it on in training is next.
- T-1007: sixth step of working out a way past where it died, second part: a go that loses a life goes back and looks. On the step the life goes, the go saves where it is, tries the small changes from the saved moment before, and either goes back there and plays the way it found (learning from every step of it, as from any other) or puts the game back where it was and carries on a life down. The life it lost stays lost to the learner and to the count: it happened. On the practice game a go that walks into the first gap goes back and jumps it. A go's result and the log now say how often it went back and whether it found a way. Not yet switched on in training: that and the measuring are next.
- T-1006: sixth step of working out a way past where it died, first part: the search itself. From a saved moment it plays what the AI did again with one small change at a time, off the record, until one stays alive and reaches ground the go had not been on, or 120 tries are used up. On the practice game it finds the jump over the first gap in 18 tries on one level and 50 on the other. Nothing starts a search yet; the go that searches when it loses a life is next.
- T-1005: fifth step of working out a way past where it died, second half. Going back counts as nothing the game did: no life lost or gained, no reward, no restart, and the screen watch goes back with it. Nothing asks a go to do it yet; the search at a death is next.
- T-1004: fifth step of working out a way past where it died, first half. - T-999: the first step of the AI working out a way past where it died instead of waiting to stumble across. - T-998: half of what the AI tries is now one button away from what it was about to do (walking right becomes walking right and jumping; with two buttons held it lets go of one), and the rest any move at all, as before. From a spot before the practice game's first gap, a try picked from all 15 moves got across in 2 to 3% of 400 tries on level 0 and under 1% on level 3; with the change, 8 to 13% and 3 to 6%. Controls with no button combinations explore exactly as before.
- T-997: what goes up when a life is lost is not progress. On the practice game "Progress goes up" was the game's mode (playing, dying, game over): it rose at every death and paid 10 points against 7.5 taken for the life, so each late go walked to the first gap and fell in, 2.5 points the better for it. Nothing in such a value's own statistics tells it from a checkpoint; when it rises does. `understanding/aftermath.py` counts, for every progress guess, how many of its rises came as something was lost (a life or health going down, or everything starting over, up to a second and a half after the rise or half a second before) and how many losses had its rise. When both are most of them (60%, with at least five of each), the guess is doubted at one review and dropped by the AI at the next; a guess a person confirmed is kept and asked about instead. Both ways are needed: progress that is often followed by a loss, such as a page crossed just before a jump the AI cannot make yet, leaves most losses without its rise. And where losses come thick and fast nothing is said at all: the first build, with a four-second window, dropped the toy racer's real checkpoint (a checkpoint every 16 frames, a life every 20), which an existing test caught, so the time near a loss must be a third or less of the time watched. On the practice bench the row is gone on both levels and the AI stops dying for points: 20 and 27 deaths in 40 goes (was 59 and 53), lives lost in the last ten goes 0.00 and 0.30 a go. It does not get further for it (121 and 172): paid honestly, it stops short of the first gap. Teaching it to cross is the next task.
- T-996: the top of a clock is not progress. On the practice game "Progress (3rd guess) goes up" was the upper byte of the level's clock: it went up every 256 frames whatever the AI did, and paid 40 points for a go spent standing still, three times what walking to the first gap earned. The lower byte had been taken for a position early on (it pauses while the player is dying, so it is not "steady" by the rule that asks for a change in 90% of steps), and the upper byte "went up each time the value below started over", which is what a lap counter does. `clock_top(low, high)` now tells the two apart from the tracker's statistics: the two bytes count together as one number, the upper has changed at least 3 times, and the lower moves by the same hop as the time before in 95% of its changes, however often it stops. The idle check at the start of a go, which already doubts a score or progress guess on a byte it sees move with nothing pressed, now looks one byte above each such byte as well: a score, progress or flag guess there that is the top of a clock is doubted the first time and dropped the second. A position's page (a byte that moves at the player's varying speed) is not touched, nor a confirmed guess, nor a lives guess. On the practice bench the row is gone on level 0 and the AI walks to the first gap again (furthest reached in the last ten goes 208, was 144); it then falls in every go (1.10 lives a go, was 0.40), because "Progress goes up" on the value that rises when a life is lost still pays 10 for dying against 7.5 taken for the life. That is the next task. Level 3 is unchanged (42): the row was not there.
- T-995: a start that gets stuck, crashes or fails says where. Boss's build wrote its first log line and never opened a window, twice, and the log had nothing more to say: it named no stage between "starting" and the Qt application, a hang or a crash inside a library runs no Python that could log it, and in the windowed build an exception that ends the launch goes to the bootloader's own error box and never to the logging hook. The start now logs each stage before it begins (loading Qt, loading the app's own code, starting the app, creating the Qt application, building the main window), so the last line is the stage that did not finish. `logbook.watch_start()` has Python's `faulthandler` write every thread's stack to `logs/start-<pid>.txt` if the window is not up after 10 seconds, and at a crash at any time; the next start copies what earlier runs left there into the log and removes the files, and a run that ends in good order leaves none. An exception from the launch is written to the log before it is raised again, once. The Windows build's smoke test now reads the log its run leaves: every stage in order, and no trace left behind. What stops the start on Boss's machine is not known yet; this is what will show it.
- T-994: a switch pays the first time it goes on (or off) in a go, and not again until the next go. The suggested "Something switches on" rows were paying at every flip, and on the practice game the two values a jump flips (on the ground, jumping) earned about 80 points a go between them, six times what getting further earned: the AI was being paid to jump on the spot. A switch is meant as an event (a door opened, a thing picked up), so `RewardService` now keeps which switches have gone which way this go (`ONCE_A_GO_KINDS`, cleared when a go starts and on connecting) and scores only the first; the unpaid flips are not tallied or announced. The row's note says "Counts the first time in a go." A person's points on such a row are kept and pay once a go too; a person's own "goes up" and "goes down" rows pay every time, as before. On the practice bench each switch row now pays 1.5 a go. The AI does not get further for it (furthest reached in the last ten goes 144 and 42, was 206 and 207): what is left paying on level 0 is a clock read as progress (40 points for a go spent standing still), and with honest pay on level 3 it stands still because it has not learned that a jump crosses the first gap. Those are the next tasks.
- T-993: the AI is paid for getting somewhere new, measured off the screen. Until now nothing paid it for getting further unless a guess about the game's memory happened to: on the practice game the guesses paid for jumping, for dying and for a clock, and nothing for walking on, so the later goes stood still. The watch now counts the new ground of each go from the picture alone (`observation/ground.py`): on a game whose screen scrolls, the camera's travel since the go began (T-992); on a screen that never scrolls, where the player stands; in steps of the player's own size, each direction on its own, each step once. A cut to another place (a quarter of the screen or more that no camera move explains), or a life lost within a few looks of a smaller jump of the screen that finds the player somewhere else on it, puts it back where the go began, so ground walked again pays nothing; a hit or a crash that leaves it where it was changes nothing. Where the camera cannot be followed (a fifth or more of its last 200 moves unmeasured, as on the racer's small screen at speed) nothing is counted at all and the log says so: counting only the moves that can be measured paid for driving slowly, which the first-demonstration test caught (the point-lover's speed fell from 0.80 to 0.43). The steps pay a new reward row, "Getting somewhere new" (Progression, 1 point a step), which appears the first time there is ground to pay for and is the person's to change, switch off or remove; the runner looks before it collects a step's reward, so the pay lands in the step that got there. On the practice bench the ground counted is as far as the game's own camera went, to within a step, in 98% and 100% of goes. The AI now walks to the first gap every go (furthest reached in the last ten goes 206 and 207, was 155 and 204) and falls in more often (38 and 50 deaths, was 11 and 24): with the suggested rewards as they are, jumping on the spot and a clock pay about ten times what new ground does and dying pays what it costs. Those rewards are the next task.
- T-992: the camera's move between two looks is measured right. The tracker searched 8 pixels either way, a limit meant for one frame, while the AI looks every 8 frames and the camera moves up to 16 pixels in that time; and the player (held still on the screen by the camera) and the strip of lives and score voted for "nothing moved". On the practice game the move was misread in about 7 of 10 looks in which the screen scrolled, which also spoilt what counts as a moving thing. Now: `estimate_scroll` takes masks of pixels to leave out and, where trying every shift would cost too much, searches coarse then fine, so a reach of 32 costs no more than 8 did; `ObjectTracker` leaves out the hidden player and the rows it has learnt are painted on the glass (`glass`), follows things on the glass as standing still, believes no camera move on the evidence of one small thing or when the best shift explains less than half of what changed, and measures "small" and "far" against the size of the screen; `ScreenWatch` lets the camera move 32 pixels between looks. The practice bench measures it against the game: right in 98% and 100% of scrolling looks on two levels (was about 27%), 100% of still ones, and a bench run takes a quarter less time because a still screen needs no search. Changed with it: the first demonstration's last step is now about three learner seeds together instead of one lucky run (its "twice as fast" claim had needed its seed moved twice), and a test of the BizHawk script that could fail on a slow machine now checks the car has driven on rather than its speed at one instant.
- T-989: the brain reads what is around it off the screen. At every look in which the AI sees itself it now reads, from the picture next to its own box and the backdrop colour alone, how much floor there is to its right and left before a drop, how far the way is clear at its own height each side, and what is under it (standing, something near below, something far below, nothing), and the brain is given those five readings first (`observation/surroundings.py`, `ScreenWatch.around`). Until now the brain saw nothing of the ground, so a gap could only be learnt by remembering where in the level it was. Also fixed: what the AI reads off the screen was added on top of a state that could already fill the brain's 32 seats, where it was the first to be dropped; twelve seats are now kept for it out of the 32 (`SCREEN_SEATS`). The practice bench measures the floor readings against the game's own level: right in 100% of about 4,900 looks on each of two levels.
- T-988: the eyes hear of every life lost, and know a fall. A life lost or a point scored on a step on which the AI did not look at the screen (every other step) was never passed to its eyes, so about half the lives lost could not be set against what was touched, and on the practice game none at all; they are now kept for the next look, and a life lost on a go's last step is told at the go's end. A life lost after the player went out of sight coming down, with nothing under it but backdrop to the bottom of the screen, is put down to the fall and not to what it touched or stood on before; coming down through the floor (a hit that throws the player off the screen) leaves the blame with the touch. The log's "On the screen so far" line and the practice bench name the falls. On the bench every fall is now seen as one (36 of 36 on level 3, 13 of 14 on level 0) and none is blamed on a touch.
- T-987: the AI knows itself by its colours. The button experiment that finds the player now also learns what it looks like (the few colours it is drawn in that the scenery and the rest of the screen do not share, grown from the slivers that moved to the whole thing), the finding keeps them with the game, and every look picks the player out of the screen afresh by those colours instead of following a track; the tracker follows everything else with the player left out of its picture, so the player is no longer one patch with the ground it stands on or the thing it touches. On the practice bench it now has itself, rightly, in 97% of looks on level 0 and 95% on level 3 (before: followed 39% and 31%, rightly 19% and 24%), with 4 kinds of thing instead of 10 and 8.
